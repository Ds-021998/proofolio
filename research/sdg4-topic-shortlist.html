The filter that actually matters

Every assessment in this course is a Business Model Canvas iteration built on real customer discovery, and the profile explicitly bans AI-generated or fabricated interview data. That single rule decides your topic more than your interests do.

It means the global framings of SDG 4 — literacy in low-income countries, refugee schooling, rural connectivity — are unavailable to you. Not because they don't matter, but because you cannot interview those people from St Lucia, twice, before September. Pick a problem whose owner is within a bus ride.

Run every candidate topic past these four questions. A “no” on the first one is fatal.

  • 01 Can we book 15 interviews in the next two weeks? Access beats passion in this course. If nobody replies by day five, the topic is already dead — you just don't know it yet.
  • 02 Do these people already work around the problem today? An existing hacky workaround — a spreadsheet, a group chat, a friend they pester — is proof the pain is real. No workaround usually means no pain.
  • 03 Can four students ship something that works by 13 November? Iteration 3 needs running code with repository logs showing individual contribution. Scope accordingly.
  • 04 Does it need ethics approval or access to minors? Interviewing school students is slow and consent-heavy. Interviewing their teachers is neither, and gets you most of the same insight.

Shortlist · ranked by fit to this course, not by importance

The five candidates

Each of these names a person, not a theme. The ranking weights interview access heavily, because that is what this course grades you on. If your team has a personal connection that changes the access picture — a parent who teaches, a friend running a bootcamp — that connection outranks my ordering.

1

Tutors and TAs drowning in marking that teaches nobody anything

SDG target 4.c · educator capacity
Who
UQ tutors, TAs and course coordinators — including the ones teaching your own courses this semester.
Suspected pain
Marking is volume work under deadline. Feedback quality collapses under it, students receive a grade instead of a lesson, and different tutors mark the same work differently.
Access
Highest of any option here. They are on campus, they hold consultation hours, and they understand what a research interview is.
Risk
The obvious answer — “AI marks it” — is crowded, politically fraught under UQ's academic integrity rules, and solution-first. If you go here, you must resist that for at least two iterations.

Sharper wedgeNot automating the mark. Making marks consistent between tutors, or turning an already-assigned mark into feedback a student can act on before the next assessment.

2

International postgrads who get the degree but not the job

SDG target 4.4 · relevant skills for employment
Who
International coursework postgrads in IT — quite possibly including several people on your own team.
Evidence
The 2024 Graduate Outcomes Survey puts international postgraduate coursework graduates at 56.1% full-time employment against 88.1% for domestic — a 32-point gap — with median salaries of $70,000 versus $100,000.
Access
Very high. Your cohort, your classes, your housemates.
Risk
The most crowded space on this list. Every second student project is a job-search tool. Without a narrow wedge you will build a worse LinkedIn.

Sharper wedgeNot “find jobs.” The specific gap between what a coursework degree certifies and what an Australian entry-level employer asks for — or how visa status silently filters applications before a human reads them.

3

Brisbane secondary teachers losing evenings to administration

SDG target 4.c · supply of qualified teachers
Who
Classroom teachers — deliberately not their students, which keeps you clear of consent complications entirely.
Evidence
AITSL's 2024 workforce data: a median 50-hour week, 18–19% spending 10+ hours weekly on administrative work, and 36% intending to leave before retirement — with 69–75% naming workload and stress as the reason.
Access
Medium. Cold outreach to schools is slow, but early-career and relief teachers are unusually willing to talk about workload. Start with anyone your team already knows.
Risk
School IT procurement is a wall — irrelevant for this course, since you are graded on discovery and a prototype, not on sales.

Sharper wedgePick one named admin task and own it. Not “teacher workload” — something like the reporting cycle, parent communication, or differentiating one lesson plan across five ability levels.

4

Neurodivergent students fighting the accommodation process, not the coursework

SDG target 4.5 · equity and inclusion
Who
University students with a Student Access Plan, or eligible for one and not using it.
Suspected pain
The administrative experience of obtaining and repeatedly invoking accommodations — disclosure to each course, evidence requirements, timing against assessment deadlines — is itself a barrier, separate from any learning difficulty.
Access
Medium, and requires care. Student union collectives and peer networks are the route in; recent Australian research on neurodivergent university students gives you a grounded starting hypothesis.
Risk
The most ethically sensitive option. If nobody on your team has lived experience here, you need genuine humility and more interviews, not fewer.

Sharper wedgeThe paperwork and disclosure loop, not the pedagogy. That is a workflow problem a student team can actually prototype.

5

Career switchers who can code but can't prove it

SDG target 4.4 · skills recognition
Who
Self-taught and bootcamp-trained adults moving into IT, reachable through Brisbane tech meetups and online communities.
Suspected pain
Competence exists; the credential to signal it doesn't. Employers can't verify, so they filter on degrees.
Access
High volume, low friction — this group loves being asked about their journey.
Risk
Every plausible solution is a two-sided marketplace, which means a cold-start problem you cannot solve by November. Ranked last for that reason alone.

Sharper wedgeOnly viable if you serve one side. Pick the learner or the employer and ignore the other until the course ends.

This week

How to pick one in five days

Do not decide by discussion. Teams that argue their way to a topic pick the one belonging to whoever argued hardest. Decide by evidence instead — the whole point of the course.

  1. Each person claims a different candidate Four teammates, four candidates. Nobody defends the one they already liked — that's how you find out whether you liked it or just thought of it first.
  2. Book two interviews each, by Friday Thirty minutes, in person or on a call. The booking itself is the test: whoever can't get a reply has just produced your most valuable finding of the week.
  3. Ask only about the past, never the future No pitching, no describing an idea. See the question bank below. Record with permission and keep the transcripts — you'll need them for the canvas and the reflections.
  4. Fill the matrix together on Monday Each person scores the candidate they investigated. Then compare against my guesses above and note where reality disagreed.
  5. Write the problem statement in one sentence “[Specific person] struggles to [specific task] because [constraint], and today they cope by [actual workaround].” If you cannot fill the last blank from something a real person said, you do not have a problem yet — go back to step two.
  6. Commit and stop looking Iteration 1 is due 4 September. Two more iterations exist precisely so you can be wrong now and correct later. Topic churn past week four is what sinks these projects.

Interview craft

What to ask, and what will ruin the data

Ask these

  • “Walk me through the last time that happened.”
  • “What did you do about it?”
  • “How long did that take you last week?”
  • “What else have you tried? Why did you stop?”
  • “Who else does this affect?”
  • “What happens if you just… don't do it?”

Never ask these

  • “Would you use an app that…” — everyone says yes to be kind.
  • “Do you think X is a problem?” — invites a polite opinion, not a fact.
  • “How much would you pay for…” — hypothetical money is imaginary.
  • “Wouldn't it be great if…” — you are now interviewing yourself.

The rule underneath all of it: people are unreliable about the future and reliable about the past. Ask what they did, not what they would do. And take the course's prohibition seriously — fabricated or AI-generated interview data fails the assessment outright, and it is easier to detect than teams expect.

Working backwards from the deadlines

The semester, in reverse

By 27 Aug Topic locked, ten interviews done Enough raw material that iteration 1 writes itself rather than being invented the night before.
4 Sep Canvas iteration 1 — hurdle Team report on customer discovery findings, plus individual reflection. Pass/fail.
Sep Second discovery round Go back to the same people with what you heard. Where you were wrong is the finding.
25 Sep Canvas iteration 2 — hurdle Expect the value proposition to have moved. A canvas identical to iteration 1 reads as no learning.
Oct Build the narrowest useful thing Commit early and often — individual contribution is assessed from repository logs, not from claims.
13 Nov Code submission & iteration 3 — hurdle Working prototype, findings report, reflection. This one you must pass outright.
