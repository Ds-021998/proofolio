The Ten-Minute Mark

COMP7110 · Direction brief · University segment

The ten-minute mark

Feedback at university isn't poor because tutors don't care. It's poor because of who marks, how long they're paid to take, and the fact that they won't be here next semester. Here is the problem, the evidence behind it, and four hypotheses your interviews can kill.

SDG 4.c primary, 4.1 secondary Status Hypothesis — unvalidated Sources 9, cited inline

Context — part one

What a large course looks like from the inside

Picture a 400-student course. Assignment 1 is due in week 5. Eight sessional tutors mark it. They are paid a fixed rate per script, hired for this semester only, and most are postgraduate students with their own deadlines. Marks are released in week 8. Assignment 2 was released in week 6 and is due in week 10.

Now look at what each person can actually see. The student receives a number and two sentences, three weeks after submitting, having already started the next task. The tutor reads sixty scripts, notices five recurring failures, writes short comments because that is what the paid time allows, and finishes. The coordinator receives a distribution of marks — a shape, with no causes attached. Next semester, seven of the eight tutors are different people.

Nobody in that picture is failing at their job. The system produces exactly what its design permits. That is what makes it a problem rather than a complaint: it is structural, it repeats at every university every semester, and nobody currently owns it.

Context — part two

The evidence

50–80% More than half of all undergraduate teaching in Australia is performed by casual staff, rising to as much as 80% of first-year teaching.[1] Rothengatter & Hil, 2013
10 min Tutors at RMIT were allocated ten minutes per paper to mark and provide feedback — described by the NTEU as half the previous allocation.[2] ABC News, 2020
~60% Instructors in a 2022 study estimated around 60% of students never access feedback left in the learning management system at all.[3] Paris, 2022
74.9% Undergraduate satisfaction in the 2025 QILT survey — the lowest since it began in 2016, and a fifth consecutive annual decline from 80.6% in 2020.[4] QILT / ABC News, 2026

Two further findings sharpen the picture. In the same casualisation survey, most casual academics reported working more hours than they were paid for, 60% did not attend faculty meetings, and many received little or no induction.[1] And in 2020, a former University of Queensland course coordinator told the ABC that staff had been instructed to skim-read assignments, or to read only the introduction and conclusion of an essay, in order to stay within paid marking time.[2]

The binding constraint on feedback quality is not effort or skill. It is a fixed and shrinking allocation of paid minutes, held by someone who was never inducted and will not teach the course again — so what they learn while marking cannot reach the student in time, and reaches nobody else at all.

Mechanism

Two loops, both cut

A feedback loop only works if what is learned returns to someone who can act on it, while they can still act. There are two such loops in a university course, and the same structure severs both.

Loop 1 — to the student · cut by timing
Student submitsWeek 5
→
Tutor marksWeeks 6–7
→
Marks releasedWeek 8
✕
Student acts on itAssignment 2 underway since week 6
Loop 2 — to the teaching team · cut by turnover
Tutor notices patternsAcross sixty scripts
✕
Reaches the coordinatorWho sees a distribution, not causes
✕
Reaches next semesterNew tutors, no induction, contract ended

Loop 1 is the one everybody complains about. Loop 2 is the more interesting one, and it explains why the same cohort misconception recurs year after year: the only people who ever see it are casual staff with no time, no forum — 60% don't attend faculty meetings[1] — and no contract that extends past November.

This is genuinely different from the equivalent problem in schools. There, knowledge is lost from one teacher's memory. At a university it is lost between people, structurally, at scale, every semester.

And the literature documents a vicious cycle on top of it

  1. Markers write less because paid time is capped and shrinking.[1][2]
  2. Students don't act on what they get — not from indifference, but because they lack strategies for using it and a firm grasp of academic discourse,[5] and because uptake requires capacities most have never been taught.[6]
  3. Markers observe the non-use and write even less. One instructor in Paris's study put it plainly: “I stopped providing comments because I think there's like two people that come out of a hundred that would pick up their papers.”[3]
  4. And the two sides aren't even optimising the same thing. Surveying 406 staff and 4,514 students, Dawson et al. found staff emphasise feedback design — timing, modality, connected tasks — while students care about the comments: usable, detailed, personalised to their own work.[7]

That last finding is the most useful thing in this brief. Both groups agree feedback exists to produce improvement, and each is working hard on a different half of the problem — which means goodwill on both sides does not close the loop by itself.

The problem statement

Written the way the course wants it

Primary — tutor side

A sessional tutor marking a large course struggles to give feedback that changes what a student does next, because they are paid for minutes per script and will not teach the course again — and today they cope by writing short generic comments, mentioning what they noticed in the tutors' group chat, and letting the rest go.

Every clause does work, and every clause can be falsified. Read them separately:

Person A sessional tutor marking a large course Not “educators.” One person, on your campus, hired this semester, whom you can email today. Also the majority of who actually teaches undergraduates in this country.[1]
Task Give feedback that changes what a student does next Not “mark the work” — that happens reliably. What fails is causing different behaviour on the next assessment. Carless and Boud frame feedback as a process of learner sense-making rather than information transmission, which is exactly this distinction.[6]
Constraint Paid for minutes per script; will not teach it again Two constraints, deliberately. The first caps what can be written now;[2] the second means nothing accumulates for later.[1] Remove either alone and the problem persists.
Workaround Short generic comments, the group chat, letting it go The weakest clause and the most important — the only one with no published evidence behind it. See H4.
Mirror — student side, same broken loop

A student struggles to do better on assignment 2 than on assignment 1, because feedback on assignment 1 arrives after they have already started assignment 2 — and today they cope by comparing marks with friends and inferring what the marker wanted.

These are not two projects. They are the same severed loop described from each end, and you will build for one of them. Which one is decided in week one by whichever side describes a real workaround when asked — not by team preference.

The hypothesis stack

Four claims, each separately killable

A single hypothesis is hard to learn from — it is either right or wrong and you don't know why. Break it into layers, so an interview can knock out one floor without collapsing the building. Take these into iteration 1 as your stated assumptions and report which survived.

Note the asymmetry deliberately built in: the first three already have published support, so your interviews are checking whether the literature holds here. The fourth has none, and only your own fieldwork can establish it.

H1

Feedback arrives too late to be usable, and students therefore do not act on it.

LiteratureJonsson's review finds students often fail to use feedback productively, lacking both strategies for engaging with it and command of the academic discourse it is written in.[5] Winstone et al.'s systematic review builds a taxonomy of what has to happen inside a learner for uptake to occur at all.[8] Instructors in Paris's study estimated ~60% never open it.[3]
Test“Tell me about the last time a mark came back. What did you do in the next two days?”
Killed ifStudents describe a concrete action they took, or say the timing was fine. Then the problem is quality, not lateness — and the project changes shape.
H2

Feedback quality is capped by the economics of casual marking, not by tutor effort or skill.

LiteratureOver half of Australian undergraduate teaching is casual, with most casuals working beyond paid hours.[1] RMIT tutors were allocated ten minutes per paper for marking and feedback; a former UQ coordinator reported staff being told to skim-read or read only introductions and conclusions.[2] Paris finds class size and workload the dominant reported barrier.[3]
Test“How long are you paid to mark one script? How long does it actually take you?”
Killed ifTutors say time isn't the binding constraint — they have enough and something else limits them. Listen very hard for what that something else is.
H3

What a marker learns about the cohort reaches neither the coordinator nor next semester.

Literature60% of casual academics did not attend faculty meetings and many received little or no induction — there is no routine forum in which such observations would travel.[1] Bloxham et al. show assessment judgement rests on tacit, individually held standards that written criteria fail to make explicit,[9] which is precisely the kind of knowledge that does not survive staff turnover.
Test“After you finished marking, did anyone ask what you noticed? Where did that go?”
Killed ifThere is a moderation or debrief meeting where this is captured and visibly acted on. Then ask whether its notes survive to next semester — that's the real test.
H4

People already work around this, informally and privately.

LiteratureNone. I could not find published evidence for this and am inferring it from the general pattern. That is not a weakness to hide — it is the one thing on this page that only your own interviews can establish, which makes it the most valuable finding available to you.
Test“Show me where your notes about this actually live.” Then ask them to open it.
Killed ifNobody has anything. No spreadsheet, no group chat, no document.

H4 is decisive. H1 to H3 can all be true while the problem is merely tolerated rather than felt — and tolerated problems do not get adopted solutions. A workaround is proof that somebody cared enough to build something themselves. If H4 dies, take the finding seriously rather than arguing with it.

Why it isn't already solved

The competitive picture

Serves loop 1 only, partially

Marking and feedback tools

Gradescope, Turnitin Feedback Studio, Canvas SpeedGrader and FeedbackFruits make writing feedback faster and more consistent. None return anything to the coordinator beyond marks, and none survive a change of teaching staff. They optimise the ten minutes; they do not question what happens after.

Wrong altitude

Learning analytics dashboards

Institutional analytics track engagement, at-risk flags and completion, working on clicks and grades — data the system already holds. They never touch the thing only a human marker knows: why the answer was wrong.

The real incumbent

The tutors' group chat

This is what holds loop 2 today, alongside a coordinator's memory and the occasional shared spreadsheet. Ephemeral, invisible to the institution, dead at semester's end. Beat that, not the enterprise products — and if H4 confirms, the chat is also where your first users already are.

Week one

What to do before the next meeting

  • Six interviews, one course. Three tutors and three students from the same course, about the same assessment. Hearing both ends of one loop beats twelve scattered conversations.
  • Ask the H-questions verbatim. Don't describe a solution. The moment you mention an idea you get politeness instead of data.
  • Record which hypothesis each interview touched and whether it strengthened or weakened it. That table is your iteration 1 findings section.
  • Decide the side at the end of the week, on evidence: whichever end produced a real workaround when asked to open it.
  • Then stop moving. Iterations 2 and 3 exist so you can be wrong within a problem, not so you can keep swapping problems.
